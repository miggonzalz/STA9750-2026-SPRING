Factory Farming and Methane Emissions - Individual Report

Author

miggonzalz

How has the balance between methane from enteric fermentation and manure management changed over time in U.S. livestock production, and what does that suggest about industrial livestock systems?

1 Introduction

This report looks at how methane emissions from U.S. livestock production changed between 1990 and 2020, and it uses those trends to project what things might look like all the way out to 2070. Methane is a really powerful greenhouse gas, trapping a lot more heat than carbon dioxide over a short period, so figuring out where it is coming from in agriculture is a big deal for climate change. A lot of times, people just look at the total amount of methane from farming, or they assume that emissions just go up naturally as the human population grows. This project shows that the real story is a bit more complicated than that.

By taking historical emissions data and combining it with U.S. population records from FAOSTAT, we can split livestock methane into its two main sources: enteric fermentation (which is just animal digestion) and manure management (how waste is handled). When you look at the data this way, a really clear split emerges. Animal digestion emissions per person have actually stayed flat or gone down a bit over the last few decades, mostly because farming has become more efficient. On the other hand, manure emissions per person have gone up steadily. This means the increase isn’t just because there are more people eating food; it is because the way livestock waste is managed has structurally changed due to large-scale, industrial farming. The report wraps up by looking at future projections, showing that if we keep going down this path, the combination of a growing population and rising waste emissions will cause a pretty significant environmental challenge down the road.

2 Data Gathering and Enviornmental Set up

Raw datasets from big international databases are usually set up for storage efficiency rather than quick analysis, so the files required some cleanup. For example, some column names had spaces instead of periods, column types were inconsistent, and the files used a “long” format where a single year had multiple rows stacked on top of each other for different variables.The goal here was to clean up the column headers, make sure numbers and years were formatted correctly, and isolate the exact rows we needed. A quick check of the raw population file showed that it didn’t just have one total population number per year. Instead, it broke the numbers down into subgroups like males, females, rural populations, and urban populations. If you don’t filter those out, R will add them all together and quadruple the true population during the merge. To fix this, I filtered the data strictly for “United States of America” and “Total Population - Both sexes”.Also, because FAOSTAT records population numbers in thousands, I multiplied that column by 1000 so it represents actual person counts. After filtering the methane data down to the key sources, enteric, manure, and the livestock total, I joined the two tables together using the Year column. Finally, I used the combined data to calculate a new metric: how many kilograms (\(kg\)) of methane are produced per person each year for each source.

To calculate this annual rate, the code uses a specific math formula that compares each year against the one right before it. First, it takes the current year’s population and subtracts the population from the previous year. This tells us the net number of new people added during that single year. Then, it takes that number of new people and divides it by the previous year’s total population. This converts the raw number of new residents into a percentage rate, showing exactly how fast the country expanded relative to its baseline size.

The interesting takeaway here is that the annual growth rate has been slowing down consistently, dropping from around 1.3% in the early 1990s to well under 0.6% by 2020. Running this calculation is a crucial detail for our analysis because it shows us the actual velocity of population expansion. Since the percentage rate of human population growth is decelerating over time, any steady acceleration we see in livestock waste emissions cannot simply be blamed on population pressure. Instead, it must be driven by operational shifts within farming infrastructure.

7 Unifiing the sources

Now that we did the individual deep dives into each FAOSTAT table, let’s analyze them together. I combined the two datasets using an inner join on the Year column so we could look at the final emissions footprint per person.

To make the resulting numbers intuitive and easy to follow, I converted the methane from kilotonnes into pounds. First, I multiplied the raw values by 1,000,000 to convert kilotonnes into kilograms, and then multiplied by 2.20462 to convert those kilograms into pounds. Finally, I divided that total weight by the human population count for each matching year. This calculation moves us away from abstract database numbers and gives us a practical baseline metric: how many pounds (\(lb\)) of livestock methane are generated per American citizen each year.

Code 
# Combine the datasets using an inner join and convert to pounds (lbs)
usa_combined <- usa_summary %>%
  inner_join(pop_clean, by = "Year") %>%
  mutate(
    # Formula: (kt * 1,000,000 * 2.20462) / Population = lbs CH4 per person
    enteric_per_capita = (digestion_methane * 1000000 * 2.20462) / Population,
    waste_per_capita   = (waste_methane * 1000000 * 2.20462) / Population,
    total_per_capita   = (total_methane * 1000000 * 2.20462) / Population
  )

library(tidyr)

# 1. Fit individual linear models based on our historical baseline data (1990-2020)
model_pop   <- lm(Population ~ Year, data = usa_combined)
model_waste <- lm(waste_per_capita ~ Year, data = usa_combined)

# 2. Setup the future milestone years we want to target
forecast_years <- tibble(
  Year = c(2025, 2030, 2050, 2070)
)

# 3. Generate predictions and convert the final total back to kilotonnes (kt)
projections <- forecast_years %>%
  mutate(
    pred_population   = predict(model_pop, newdata = forecast_years),
    pred_waste_capita = predict(model_waste, newdata = forecast_years),
    
    # Formula: (Population * lbs_per_capita) / (1,000,000 * 2.20462) to get back to kt CH4
    pred_total_waste  = (pred_population * pred_waste_capita) / (1000000 * 2.20462)
  )

To figure out the total future impact, the model takes the projected population and multiplies it by the projected pounds of waste per person for each milestone year. Because global databases track emissions in kilotonnes, the code divides that giant total by 1,000,000 and then by 2.20462. This reverses our earlier math, moving the data out of individual pounds and returning it to standard kilotonnes (\(kt\)) so it can be plotted seamlessly next to our historical records
